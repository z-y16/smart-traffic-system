===============================================================================
HOW THE WHOLE SMART TRAFFIC SYSTEM WORKS
The full tour, from "a camera looking at a road" to "the traffic light changes"
===============================================================================


THE ONE-SENTENCE VERSION
------------------------

It's a traffic warden who never blinks, never gets bored, and writes down
everything: it watches a road through a camera, works out how many vehicles
there are and how fast they're going, decides whether the road is busy, changes
the traffic light to suit, spots ambulances, tells some real electronics what
to do, and keeps a diary of all of it.


IT'S REALLY TWO PROGRAMS
------------------------

    THE CV NODE  (broadcast_server.py)
        The eyes and the hands. It needs the camera and a good graphics card.
        It does all the watching, thinking, and controlling.

    THE DASHBOARD  (SmartTrafficSystem/)
        The control room screen. Just a website. Shows charts and buttons.

They're kept separate on purpose, because they need different things. The node
needs to be where the camera is. The dashboard just needs a browser — so it can
run on a laptop in a different room, or a different building.

You start both at once by double-clicking START HERE.bat.


===============================================================================
THE JOURNEY OF ONE PICTURE
===============================================================================

Every single frame of video goes through this whole chain. Let's follow one.


STEP 0 — WHERE THE PICTURE COMES FROM

Three choices, and the system doesn't care which:
    * a camera plugged in
    * a video file (you can drag one in from the dashboard while it's running)
    * a road camera somewhere on the internet (a stream URL)

One rule: the camera must be STILL. On a pole, a bridge, a gantry. If the
camera itself moves, then everything in the picture appears to move, and the
system would think parked cars were driving. Everything below depends on this.

THE CLOCK TRICK (this one is cleverer than it sounds)

Speed = distance ÷ TIME. So which clock do you use?

If you use the real clock on the wall, you get the wrong answer for a video.
Say the video was filmed at 30 pictures per second, but your computer is slow
and only manages 20. Now every car seems to take longer to cross the screen
than it really did, and you report speeds that are far too slow. Use a fast
computer instead and it races through the video and reports silly fast speeds.

That's ridiculous — the cars in the video drove at one speed, and it has
nothing to do with which computer is watching them.

So a video gets its OWN clock, which ticks one frame at a time no matter how
fast or slow the computer is. We tested this by driving a fake car at exactly
16.2 km/h through a real video at four different speeds — normal, double speed,
half speed, and as fast as the computer could go. All four answers came out at
16.2 km/h. They agreed exactly.


STEP 1 — FIND THE VEHICLES  (YOLO)

We use a model called YOLO (yolo11m). You give it a picture and it draws a box
round everything it recognises and names it: car, motorcycle, bus, truck.

Three things we do to make it better at this job:

    * We show it a BIG picture (1280 pixels, not 640). This is the single
      biggest improvement for far-away and night-time vehicles. Measured on our
      night footage: the small size found 15.6 vehicles per picture, the big
      size found 19.6.
    * We use "FP16", a maths shortcut that makes it about twice as fast — which
      is what pays for the big picture.
    * We tell it to only look for vehicles. It's not allowed to accidentally
      decide a person or a traffic light is a car.

Interesting: we tried a NEWER model (yolo12m) and it was worse for us — 33%
slower AND found fewer vehicles. So we kept yolo11m. Newer isn't always better.


STEP 2 — STICK A NAME TAG ON EACH ONE  (tracking)

YOLO has no memory. Every picture, it starts from scratch. So it can tell you
"there are 5 cars", but not "that's the SAME red car from a second ago".

A tracker (BoT-SORT) fixes that by putting a numbered sticker on each vehicle
and keeping it there frame after frame. Without this you couldn't measure speed
(you need the same car in two places), and you couldn't count vehicles properly
(you'd count the same car hundreds of times).

Two safety rules:
    * A vehicle must survive 3 frames before we believe in it. This stops a
      one-frame flicker from being counted as a car.
    * We take a VOTE on what kind of vehicle it is, over the last 30 frames,
      and the winner has to properly beat the current answer. Otherwise a van
      would flicker "car, truck, car, truck" forever.


STEP 3 — HOW FAST IS IT GOING?

Here's the problem: the camera sees PIXELS, and pixels aren't metres. A car far
away moves only a few pixels while going the same speed as a close car that
moves loads of pixels. So "pixels per second" is not a speed at all.

We need a way to turn pixels into real metres. Two ways:

    AUTOMATIC (works instantly, roughly ±25% accurate)
    We know a real car is about 1.5 m tall and a bus about 3.2 m. So we look at
    how tall the box is in pixels, and that tells us how many metres one pixel
    is worth AT THAT EXACT SPOT. Clever bit: every vehicle carries its own
    scale, so near and far are both handled automatically.

    CALIBRATED (do it once, ±5% accurate)
    Run calibrate_speed.py, click 4 corners of a rectangle on the road, and
    type in how big it really is in metres. A lane is about 3.5 m wide — easy
    to look up. Now the system knows the real shape of the road.

Then, to actually measure:
    1. We use the BOTTOM-MIDDLE of the box — where the tyres touch the road.
       That's the only part of the vehicle that's actually on the ground.
    2. We measure over 0.8 seconds, not between two frames. Boxes wobble a bit
       every frame, and we don't want to report wobble as speed.
    3. Anything over 200 km/h is thrown away — that's not a car, that's the
       tracker mixing up two vehicles.

Tested: a fake car scripted to drive at exactly 18, 36 and 72 km/h was measured
at 18.0, 36.0 and 72.0.

IMPORTANT: a calibration belongs to ONE camera in ONE position. Move the
camera, and you have to do it again.


STEP 4 — IS THE ROAD BUSY?  (the congestion index)

We turn "how bad is it" into one number between 0 and 1. It's made of two
ingredients:

    HOW SLOW is everyone?  (60% of the score)
    HOW FULL is the road?  (40% of the score)

For "how full", not all vehicles are equal. A bus takes up way more room than a
motorbike, so they're weighted:

        motorcycle = 0.5        car = 1        bus = 3        truck = 3

Then the number becomes a word: FREE, MODERATE, or HEAVY.

Two rules stop it saying stupid things:

    * An empty road is never congested. Sounds obvious! But the first version
      remembered the last speed it saw, so an empty road could still be
      reported as HEAVY traffic. Now, zero vehicles = zero congestion, full stop.

    * One slow car is not a traffic jam — it's one slow driver. So "everyone is
      slow" only counts once there are actually about 3+ vehicles around to be
      slow together.

There's also a "stickiness" rule so the word doesn't flicker between MODERATE
and HEAVY every single frame when the number is sitting right on the line —
because the LEDs and the servo would physically chatter back and forth.


STEP 5 — IS IT AN EMERGENCY VEHICLE?

Two helpers — CLIP reads the paintwork, the siren detector watches for a
flashing beacon. This has its own file:

        --> see HOW_EMERGENCY_DETECTION_WORKS.txt

Short version: a flashing beacon means it's on a call right now. Paintwork
alone means it's an ambulance, but possibly a parked one.


STEP 6 — DECIDE THE TRAFFIC LIGHT

The light is "demand-responsive" — it serves traffic when there IS traffic:

        HEAVY      ->  GREEN   (let the queue clear)
        MODERATE   ->  GREEN
        FREE       ->  RED     (nobody to serve, so don't hold a green)
        any change ->  YELLOW for 5 seconds first

Two rules make it behave like a real traffic light:

    * ONCE YELLOW STARTS, IT FINISHES. All five seconds, every time, and it
      always ends up at the colour it was heading for. Real traffic lights never
      abandon an amber halfway through, and neither does this.
    * MINIMUM WAIT. Green and red are held at least 3 seconds. Without this,
      traffic changing during the yellow could produce a green light that lasts
      a single frame.

We tested this by flipping the traffic level every half second for two whole
minutes. The light never once produced a momentary phase, and never broke the
red->yellow->green->yellow->red order.

A SURPRISING DECISION: an ambulance does NOT take over the traffic light.

Why not? Because emergency vehicles are legally allowed to drive through a red
light anyway. Grabbing the light wouldn't help the ambulance at all — it would
just mess everything up for everyone else. So instead, a special LED strip
lights up to tell nearby drivers to MOVE OVER and make space, and the LCD
screen names the vehicle. The traffic light carries on doing its normal job.


STEP 7 — TELL THE ELECTRONICS  (the Arduino)

The node talks over a USB cable to real hardware:

        the traffic light LEDs (red / yellow / green)
        an LCD screen (shows the status, names the emergency vehicle)
        a servo motor (a barrier arm that moves with the light phase)
        an emergency LED strip (pin 6 — the "make space" one)
        a speed bump that can raise and lower
        a lane divider that can give more lanes to the busier direction

Only the node is allowed to hold the USB port — on Windows two programs
genuinely cannot open the same port at once. So when you press a button on the
dashboard, it doesn't talk to the Arduino itself; it asks the node to do it.
That's exactly what lets the dashboard live on another computer.

And when a command is sent, the reply says whether it actually reached the
wire — so if someone unplugged the Arduino, you SEE that, instead of the button
pretending it worked.


STEP 8 — WRITE IT ALL DOWN

Two diaries, because they answer two different questions:

    TODAY'S DIARY  (traffic_history.csv / .xlsx)
        Just this run. This is what you show in a demo. Starts fresh each time
        — but the old one is safely moved into sessions/ first, never deleted.

    THE FOREVER DIARY  (traffic_all_sessions.csv / .xlsx)
        Every run ever, added to and never reset.

Each one has several sheets: a live status, every measurement, one row per
vehicle that passed through (with its class, how long it took, and its average
and top speed), and a summary of the whole session.


STEP 9 — SHOW IT ON SCREEN  (the dashboard)

A website with charts that each answer a real question:

    Demand by Hour ......... When is this road busiest?
    Speed vs Congestion .... What does being busy actually cost in journey time?
    Signal Behaviour ....... How is the light spending its time?
    Distribution ........... Is it always bad, or occasionally terrible?
                             (an average can't tell those two apart!)
    Session Comparison ..... Is it getting worse over time?

Every chart comes with a "how to read this" note, and there's a panel that
turns the numbers into plain sentences — the busiest hour, whether jams are
constant or occasional, how much speed is lost when the road fills up.


===============================================================================
THE BIT TO BE PROUDEST OF
===============================================================================

Almost every design decision here exists because something went wrong first,
got measured, and got fixed. That's the real story of the project:

    "Empty road, HEAVY traffic"     -> now zero vehicles means zero congestion
    "Slow computer, slow cars"      -> videos got their own clock
    "One slow driver = a jam"       -> speed only counts with 3+ vehicles
    "The light flickered"           -> stickiness + minimum wait added
    "29% of cars called police"     -> the unfair vote was made fair
    "A red car under a streetlight" -> the switch-vs-slide test
    "Newer model must be better"    -> measured it; it wasn't; kept the old one

And it's all held down by TESTS — 769 automatic checks, and right now every
single one of them passes:

        217  the vision pipeline
        124  hardware commands, the web server, the launcher
        113  video sources, uploads and playback
        100  the dashboard buttons and controls
         90  the analytics maths
         69  all 8 dashboard pages render
         56  the traffic light timing

If someone changes something and accidentally breaks the traffic light timing
or the speed maths, the tests catch it immediately instead of at the demo.

(On Windows you must run them with PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 set first. Otherwise
two of them break — not because anything is wrong, but because the old Windows
console can't print the emoji on a dashboard button.)


===============================================================================
HOW FAST IS IT?
===============================================================================

Measured on the laptop's graphics card (an RTX 4050), on real 1080p footage:

    Finding + tracking the vehicles ....... 29 ms   (about 66% of the work)
    Reading the paintwork (CLIP) ........... 5 ms   (about 12%)
    Watching for flashing beacons .......... 10 ms  (about 22%)
    ---------------------------------------------
    Everything, start to finish ............ 45 ms  = about 22 pictures/second

In daylight it's faster — about 28 pictures a second — because there are fewer
beacons to measure and the paint is easier to read.

Two things worth knowing:

    * Finding the vehicles is two-thirds of the work. So if you ever want to
      make it faster, that's the only part worth touching. Making the maths
      faster would save nothing you could measure.

    * Making the PICTURE bigger helps a lot. Making the MODEL bigger doesn't.
      Going from a small picture to a big one finds about 5 more vehicles per
      frame. Going from a small model to a big one at the same picture size
      finds... nothing extra. Sometimes slightly fewer! The thing that limits
      us is how many pixels a far-away car takes up, not how clever the model
      is.

All the numbers, and how they were measured, are in METRICS.md.
