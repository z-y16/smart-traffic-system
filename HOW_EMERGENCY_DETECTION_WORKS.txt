===============================================================================
HOW OUR SYSTEM SPOTS AN AMBULANCE
Two helpers: CLIP (the "what is it?" helper) and the Siren Detector (the
"is it on a call right now?" helper)
===============================================================================


THE PROBLEM WE HAD
------------------

Our vehicle detector is called YOLO. YOLO is very good at one thing: pointing
at things in a picture and saying "that's a car", "that's a truck", "that's a
bus".

But YOLO has never been taught the word "ambulance". Nobody ever showed it one
and told it the name. So when an ambulance drives past, YOLO looks at it and
says... "truck". A police car? "car". A fire engine? "truck".

It sees the vehicle perfectly. It just doesn't know what KIND it is.

That's the hole. And we fill it with two helpers who are good at two totally
different things.


===============================================================================
HELPER 1 — CLIP  (the "what does it LOOK like?" helper)
===============================================================================

WHO IS CLIP

Imagine a kid who has read 400 MILLION picture books. Every single page had a
photo and a sentence underneath saying what the photo was. After all those
books, that kid has a really good feel for which words go with which pictures.

That kid is CLIP. The exact version we use is called ViT-B/16.

Here's the important bit: WE DIDN'T TEACH IT ANYTHING. We never showed it our
camera, our road, or our videos. It already knew what an ambulance looks like
before it ever met us. This is called "zero-shot" — zero lessons needed.

That's why it works in any country. An Egyptian ambulance, a British one, an
American one — it was in the picture books.


HOW WE ASK CLIP A QUESTION

We cut out the vehicle from the picture (just that vehicle, nothing else).
Then we hand CLIP the cut-out plus a list of 38 sentences, like:

    "a photo of an ambulance"
    "a photo of a police car"
    "a photo of a fire engine"
    "a photo of an ordinary family car"
    "a photo of a delivery van"
    ... and so on

CLIP scores every sentence and tells us which one fits best.


THE SNEAKY BUG WE FIXED (this one matters)

The first version asked the question in an unfair way. It added up the scores
of ALL 14 emergency sentences and compared that to the 15 ordinary ones.

Think of a vote where 14 people are on one team and 15 on the other, but
everybody is just guessing. You'd expect roughly half the votes each way —
about 0.48. And our "yes it's an emergency" line was drawn at 0.45.

So any blurry, dark, useless little picture that CLIP couldn't read at all
scored 0.48 and got called a POLICE CAR. On our night video, that meant
88 out of 299 vehicles — 29% of all traffic — were wrongly flagged.

The fix: make it a fair one-on-one fight instead. Just
"best emergency guess" versus "ordinary vehicle". Now that same useless blurry
picture scores 0.25, nowhere near the line. Problem gone.


THE OTHER SAFETY RULES

* Too dark or too blurry? Don't even ask. If the cut-out is nearly black
  (brightness under 28) or completely flat and foggy (contrast under 12), we
  skip it. CLIP will happily give you a confident answer about a picture of
  nothing, so we just don't show it pictures of nothing.

* You have to say it three times. One lucky guess isn't enough — we need at
  least 3 checks agreeing before we believe it.

* Sticky yes, sticky no. To turn the flag ON you need a score of 0.70. To turn
  it back OFF it has to drop all the way to 0.50. Like a door that needs a
  firm push to open and a firm pull to close — so it doesn't rattle back and
  forth on every frame.

* Lazy on purpose. We re-check each vehicle every 5 frames. If it's clearly
  just a boring normal car, we get lazier and lazier about it (up to every 60
  frames) and spend our time on the interesting ones instead.


WHAT CLIP ADDED TO OUR SYSTEM

    * It gives the vehicle its NAME. YOLO says "truck", CLIP says "ambulance".
    * It needs no training and no photos from us. It just works.
    * It works on cameras and countries we've never seen.
    * In DAYLIGHT it is the only helper that works at all (see below).


===============================================================================
HELPER 2 — THE SIREN DETECTOR  (the "is the light FLASHING?" helper)
===============================================================================

BIG SURPRISE FIRST: this one is not an AI at all. There's no brain in it,
nothing learned, nothing trained. It's just careful maths about light and
time. It's a kid with a stopwatch, staring at a lamp and counting blinks.


STEP 1 — LOOK IN THE RIGHT PLACE

Beacons sit on the ROOF. So we only look at the top part of the vehicle, and
we stretch our box 18% ABOVE the roof too, because a bright lamp glows out
past the metal it's sitting on.

This also keeps us away from the brake lights down at the bottom — which are
red, and which would fool us constantly if we looked there.


STEP 2 — IS IT REALLY RED OR REALLY BLUE?

Not "a bit reddish". Properly red.

    For RED: the green must be less than 0.55 of the red.
             (This is what throws out ORANGE indicator lights.)
    For BLUE: the red must be less than 0.70 of the blue.

And it must be BRIGHT — at least 165 out of 255. Here's why that number:
a real beacon is a LIGHT SOURCE, and light sources nearly blind the camera.
Measured on our night video, real beacon pixels sat at 200-255, while ordinary
bluish car paint and reflections sat at 130-155. So we drew the line between
them at 165.


STEP 3 — THE STOPWATCH (this is the clever part)

A red light that is just... on... is a tail light. A beacon SWITCHES.

So over a 3.4-second window we check:

    * Did it blink at least 3 times?
    * Was the speed between 1 and 6 blinks a second? (Real beacons are
      regulated by law. Slower than 1 is not a beacon. Faster than 6 is
      camera noise.)
    * Did it properly go OUT between blinks, not just dim a bit?


STEP 4 — THE BEST TRICK OF ALL: SWITCH vs SLIDE

This one rule catches the false alarms that every other rule misses.

A lamp is a SWITCH. It is either fully ON or fully OFF. It is basically never
caught halfway.

A red car driving under a street lamp is a SLIDE. It gets brighter, brighter,
brightest, dimmer, dimmer. It spends LOTS of its time halfway.

So we count how many of our measurements landed in the "halfway" zone. If more
than 15% are halfway, it's a car sliding under a light, not a beacon.

How well does it work? On our night footage the real beacon scored 0.00
(never once caught halfway) and every single false alarm scored between
0.18 and 0.43. It separates them completely.


WHEN IT SAYS NO, IT TELLS YOU WHY

    too-dim ................ no light bright enough to be a lamp
    steady-light ........... there's a coloured light, but it never goes off
    shallow-modulation ..... it dims but never goes out (probably a reflection)
    gradual-not-switched ... it slides instead of switching (the trick above)
    not-repeating .......... it blinked, but fewer than 3 times
    implausible-rate ....... blinking too fast or too slow to be legal
    red-below-roofline ..... red light, but down on the body = brake lights
    window-too-short ....... the vehicle wasn't on screen long enough


===============================================================================
HOW THE TWO HELPERS WORK TOGETHER
===============================================================================

They answer two DIFFERENT questions, and keeping them separate is the whole
design:

    CLIP asks:   "What kind of vehicle is that?"
    Siren asks:  "Is it on an emergency call RIGHT NOW?"

Why does that difference matter? Because an ambulance parked outside a
hospital is still an ambulance — but you should NOT stop all the traffic for
it. It's not going anywhere. It's having its lunch break.

So the rule is:

    Beacon flashing  -> "AMBULANCE - PRIORITY"   -> actually hold the junction
    Only the paint   -> "AMBULANCE"              -> say so on screen, but
                                                    DON'T hold the junction

And they help each other out:

    A BLUE flashing light can only ever be police. Case closed, no help needed.
    A RED flashing light could be an ambulance OR a fire engine. So we ask
    CLIP which one it is, because only the paint can tell them apart.


===============================================================================
THE DAYLIGHT PROBLEM (be ready to be asked about this)
===============================================================================

In daylight, most vehicles get flagged by CLIP and not by the siren detector.
That is NOT a bug. Here's why:

In bright light, a beacon that's strong enough to see at all completely
overwhelms the camera sensor and burns out to pure WHITE. And white is not
red, and white is not blue — so our colour rules correctly reject it.

Measured on a real daylight ambulance:
    beacon colour score .... 0.04   (the camera saw white, not red)
    livery (paint) score ... 0.95   (CLIP read it perfectly)

So in the daytime, CLIP is carrying the whole thing. At night, the siren
detector takes over and becomes the strong one. Together they cover both.

WHAT WE ACTUALLY GOT ON THE YOUTUBE TEST VIDEO
===============================================================================

Video: "Police Cars Fire Trucks And Ambulances Responding Compilation Part 12"
11 minutes 43 seconds, all of it, no ROI, no speed, nothing cherry-picked.

    frames looked at ................ 21,083
    vehicles tracked ................ 2,511
    flagged as emergency ............ 166   (6.6%)
    ...which is about ............... 96 real vehicles (the rest is the
                                      tracker giving one vehicle two numbers)
    beacon-confirmed (PRIORITY) ..... 21

    by type: 60 fire trucks, 59 ambulances, 47 police cars

Confidence was mostly 0.93 to 1.00 — very sure.

The 21 beacons it DID read in daylight were measured blinking between
1.2 and 4.8 times per second, which is exactly the legal range for real
beacons. Blue ones on police cars, red ones on fire engines and ambulances.

The number to be proud of is 6.6%. It looked at 2,511 vehicles and pointed at
166 of them. A system that shouted "AMBULANCE!" at everything would prove
nothing at all.


BE HONEST ABOUT THIS BIT
------------------------

6.6% is NOT the same as "6.6% of our answers were wrong" or "93.4% correct".
Nobody has sat down and checked all 166 by hand. We looked at a sample of the
saved pictures and most were obvious ambulances and fire engines — but a couple
were blurry cut-outs from busy traffic where a real ambulance was sitting just
behind the car in the box.

So the right thing to say is: "it flagged 166 out of 2,511, and the ones we
checked were nearly all correct." Not: "it is 93% accurate." If someone asks
for a proper accuracy score, the honest answer is that it would take a person
labelling a few hundred frames by hand first — and that hasn't been done yet.

Being able to say exactly what you HAVEN'T proved is part of doing this well.


HOW FAST IS IT?
---------------

Reading the paintwork costs about 5 milliseconds per picture at night, and
about 1 in daylight. Watching for beacons costs about 10 at night, 5 in
daylight. So both emergency helpers together are under a quarter of the work —
finding the vehicles in the first place is two-thirds of it.
